Background

A statewide trend of school closures reaches Alachua County

A statewide trend of closing schools has reached Alachua County. On March 12, 2026, the ACPS school board voted to move forward with permanently closing Stephen Foster Elementary, while Williams and Alachua are set to close at the end of the 26-27 school year. Other schools such as Lincoln Middle and Oak View Middle are being transformed into K-8 schools to incorporate students from underenrolled elementaries.

Over the past few years, the school district has allocated less state funding to schools in the East Gainesville area, due to declining enrollment rates and birth rates within these school zones. The closing of select elementary and middle schools are part of an ongoing effort to reduce costs and spend money more efficiently across the district.

Historically, schools on the east side of town (compared to other schools) are underperforming academically and report higher rates of behavioral incidents. Given the county's existing funding priorities, our tool is designed to help the public better understand the external factors and conditions that may be associated with these differences.

The goal of our work is not to attribute these outcomes (in East GNV) to the schools or the communities they serve, but to identify factors that may warrant further attention when considering resource allocation and policy options. This information can help the county explore alternatives to school closures, including targeted investments and other interventions that address the underlying conditions affecting school performance.

Sources: Gainesville Sun, WUFT News, The Independent Florida Alligator

What is our tool?

Widen what policymakers look at before they move a line or close a school

Strengths of our tool

Our tool would assist policymakers in making educated decisions regarding funding that address other underlying factors beyond enrollment numbers and academic performance. We intend to encourage a more holistic understanding of why schools in certain areas may be experiencing difficulties in either bringing students to campus and/or retaining students performing well in academic settings. Some factors we have identified meriting discussion are socio-economic status, percentage of Black and Hispanic students in a school, and the availability of qualified teachers.

Limitations

We are essentially pushing for policy makers to increase their scope of factors to consider when making crucial decisions related to funding. However, in practical terms, ACPS doesn't seem to have a standardized way to compile data from these different data sources. We also have a rudimentary understanding of what factors policy makers want to consider.

The three predictors this tool tracks

Cost per student — funding
Student population per school — enrollment
Students eligible for free & reduced lunch — socio-economic status

I. What the money says

Per-pupil funding, school by school, east of 441

Florida's funding follows the student. A school does not get a budget because it is poor or rich; it gets a per-student amount, multiplied by however many students show up. So the honest first question is not are East Gainesville schools shortchanged per head — the bars below show per-head amounts that are roughly in line with, and sometimes above, the district's other campuses. The honest question is what happens to the total, when the heads keep leaving.

The claim this project can actually support is correlation: enrollment drains from these schools, and the money left with it. Whether choice programs caused the drain — or merely followed it — is a causal claim the data here cannot prove, and this paper won't pretend otherwise. — Framing note, project methodology

II. The students who left

A decade of enrollment drift, east of 441

Each panel below is one school's real enrollment, year by year, from FL DOE membership files. The amber dot is that school's peak; the teal dot is today. What a school loses between those two dots, it loses in per-pupil dollars — this is the mechanism by which choice and magnet seats, plus declining birthrates and housing turnover, turn into thinner staffing and older textbooks on the east side.

III. Empty seats are expensive

The 2026 redraw inherited buildings running half-empty

After the first comprehensive rezoning since 2003, the consultant's own numbers show East Gainesville elementary zones filled at a fraction of capacity — Lake Forest at 45%, Shell at 65%, Metcalfe at 61%. A school at half capacity is a school whose fixed costs — building, staff, administration — are spread over half the per-pupil revenue. Rezoning moved the lines. It did not move the money formula.
